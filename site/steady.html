Fitness apps lose almost everyone within weeks

And the mechanics meant to keep people fit poorly with those who train for health. Streaks, leaderboards and performance targets serve people chasing results. When a sick child or a late meeting breaks the plan, the app records a failure.

Adults who train for health need a plan that bends when life does, and feedback that treats a missed session as information, not a lapse. Existing apps ask them to bend to the plan.

Working problem statement

What the evidence says, and what it means for the product

The research agrees on one point: motivation that comes from the person (autonomy, competence, a plan that fits their life) predicts long-term exercise better than external pressure.

  1. Broken streaks sap motivation

    Streaks raise motivation while they last; a broken one reduces it, and highlighting the break can backfire.

    → No daily streak. Measure weeks, and let a missed week be absorbed, not announced.

    Silverman & Barasch 2023, INSEAD

  2. Competition adds stress

    In a preregistered experiment, competition-based gamification raised stress and negative social dynamics; a self-exploration version reduced them.

    → No leaderboards in v1. Private reflection instead.

    Bai, Kybernetes

  3. Autonomy predicts adherence

    Across 66 studies, autonomous motivation and competence predicted exercise; intrinsic motivation best predicted long-term adherence.

    → Users shape and edit their own plan, and see capacity grow.

    Teixeira et al. 2012

  4. One miss does not break a habit

    New habits took a median of 66 days to become automatic, and missing one opportunity did not materially affect it.

    → The first 12 weeks are the habit window. A missed session needs no penalty or repair flow.

    Lally et al. 2009

  5. Guilt is a named barrier for working parents

    Parents cite family responsibilities, guilt, scheduling and work as barriers; being active with the children helps.

    → Plan around the family calendar, and write copy that never adds guilt.

    Mailey et al. 2014

Gap in the evidence: most of these studies are short and use general or student samples. None tests an adaptive weekly goal for parents, which is what the next round of research is for.

For people whose week is the variable

Adults around 28–50 who train two to four times a week across strength, cycling and running, for health and enjoyment rather than results. Not for racers or leaderboard fans; Strava, Runna and Fitbod serve them well.

Seven rules the product has to keep

Each principle became concrete product rules. A feature that breaks one needs an explicit decision to proceed.

  1. P1Plans bend, people don’t

    Every session can be shortened, swapped or moved in two taps, and the plan regenerates.

  2. P2Weeks, not days

    Goals, summaries and rewards use the calendar week. No daily streak anywhere.

  3. P3Celebrate showing up, in any size

    A 10-minute minimum session counts toward the week.

  4. P4Absence is neutral

    No loss framing or streak-break alerts. Returning always starts with a welcome.

  5. P5Compare with your past self only

    No leaderboards, rankings or public sharing in v1.

  6. P6Quiet by default

    At most three notification types, each user-controlled, none reporting a miss.

  7. P7Honest about limits

    No medical claims, calorie goals or body targets. Rest is a valid, visible part of the plan.

How it works

A gap lowers nothing you already earned

The unit is the week, and each week closes in one of four states. Steady shows “steady weeks in the last 12”: a count over a window, not a consecutive run, so it can never reset to zero. Tap a week to change it.

Three notifications, no more

  • Weekly check-inOn the day the user picks. Skipped if the check-in is done.
  • Session reminderOpt-in per session. Never about a missed one.
  • Re-entry welcomeAfter 7 days without a session. Sent once, then at most every 14 days, then it stops.

Ignore or opt out of two in a row, and that type stops until it is turned back on.

A rules-based planner you can explain

Version one uses rules, not machine learning, so every session can carry a one-line reason, every rule can be tested on fixtures, and a coach can review the parameters before beta.

MVP and the cut line

The MVP is the smallest set that can run a 12-week beta on iOS: onboarding, check-in, adaptive plan, Today, session logging, Apple Health and Calendar, the consistency view, missed-week recovery, three notifications and a subscription.

If time runs short, the Watch companion goes first, then calendar sync. The check-in, the recovery flow and the consistency view never go: they carry the core hypotheses.

  1. R1Capacity first

    Never more than the free windows, never less than the minimum week.

  2. R2Energy changes intensity first

    Low energy shortens sessions and keeps the week’s shape.

  3. R3Hard, then easy

    No hard sessions back to back; no heavy legs before a long ride.

  4. R4A missed session moves

    To a free window this week, or it drops. Never doubled up.

  5. R5A missed week restarts lighter

    Then builds back gradually.

  6. R6Progress only on completion

    Otherwise the plan holds.

  7. R7Pause freezes progression

    Illness, injury or travel, then a scaled restart.

  8. R8Weather fallback

    Rain on ride day offers another day, indoors or a run.

08 · Today

One card for the day, with a reason

The headline sets the bar: two sessions make a steady week. Every session says why it is where it is, so an automatic plan reads as considered rather than arbitrary. Any day can be swapped for rest without a reason.

12 · Weekly check-in

Three questions, about 30 seconds

Energy, availability (pre-filled from the calendar) and known disruptions. Low energy changes intensity before frequency, so a tired week keeps its shape. Whether people will really do this weekly is hypothesis H3.

09 · Week plan

Plans bend in two taps

Move, shorten or rest, none of which asks why. One switch collapses the whole week to its minimum. The plan regenerates around every change and never lands on a calendar conflict.

14 · Welcome back

The moment after a miss decides retention

So it gets the best design effort, not a reminder. No loss message, no red state: a welcome and a choice of how big the week should be, with a gentle restart as the default. Whether this brings people back more often than a reminder is H1, the first thing to test.

17 · Journey

Compared only with your past self

The history shows showing up, not performance. Break weeks are quiet dashed outlines, and the notes worth keeping are about patterns, like coming back after two quiet weeks.

Decisions and what they cost

  • Weeks, not days

    A week that met a minimum is a more honest and forgiving unit, and it matches WHO’s weekly guidance.

    Cost: less of the daily pull that streaks create. If users ask, an opt-in daily view can be added (E2).

  • Absence is neutral

    A missed week is a quiet gap. Paused weeks don’t count at all.

    Cost: no loss aversion to lean on. H1 tests whether a welcome beats a reminder.

  • Quiet notifications

    Three types, user-controlled, none about a miss.

    Cost: fewer re-engagement touches. The opt-out rate is a guardrail, not an afterthought.

  • A rules-based planner

    Explainable, testable on fixtures and reviewable by a coach.

    Cost: less personalisation than a learned model. That can come once beta data exists.

  • Paywall after the first session

    People should feel a plan that fits their week, and ideally a recovery, before they are asked to pay. More than half of trial cancellations happen on day one.

    Cost: some users leave before seeing a price. Timing is experiment E5; trial length is E4.

Next: find out if it is true

Eight hypotheses hold the product up. The riskiest three are tested first, before any build is committed.

  • H1A welcome with a smaller plan brings people back after a missed week more often than a reminder.
  • H2A weekly consistency goal motivates this segment more than a daily streak.
  • H4One plan across three sports is worth paying for over single-sport apps.
